ABSTRACT
r
Based on a sample of 5,558 stock-for-stock mergers and acquisitions (M&As) between 2004 and 2020, we show
that, in the year before the M&A announcement, stock ebidders manipulate the narrative content of corporate
disclosures by inflating the tone of earnings press releases in order to curtail the target acquisition cost.
Furthermore, tone inflation is more pronounced in firms executing larger-scale deals, firms with weaker
e
historical performance, and firms with limited monitoring mechanisms in place. We also find that, although tone
inflation propels an upswing in the bidder’s stock prices and leads to a reduction in the overall target acquisition
cost, it exposes the bidder to unfavorable long-pterm returns after the merger and acquisition announcement. We
subject our analyses to an exhaustive array of robustness and endogeneity tests, which consistently affirm the
validity of our conclusions. By distorting the narrative tone, stock bidders compromise the quality and reliability
of information provided to stakeholders, undermining the transparency and trust upon which effective financial
t
reporting relies.

1. Introduction
e
Although mergers and acquisitions (M&As) activity has significantly grown over the last decade, there
w
remains much about the M&A process that we need to comprehend, including the implication of the
method of payment on managers’ opportunistic behavior.1 The use of stocke as a method of payment is
also known to create a strong incentive for managers to manipulate earnings to positively influence the
i
stock price, and therefore reduce the target’s acquisition cost (Ericksovn and Wang, 1999; Louis, 2004).
However, it remains unclear whether stock bidders also strategiceally vary the narrative content of their
corporate disclosures. Given the substantial influence of qualitative information on the firm’s stock price
r
(Henry, 2008; Henry and Leone, 2016; Fu, Wu, and Zhang, 2021; Arslan-Ayaydin, Boudt, and
r
Thewissen, 2016), it is essential to examine whether the use of stock as a form of payment leads managers
e
to manipulate the content of qualitative information in corporate disclosures.
In the majority of M&As, the acquiring efirm buys the target’s assets using cash. However, in
some cases, the acquiring firm uses its stock in exchange for the target’s stock. In stock-for-stock
p
mergers, acquiring firms exchange their stock against the stock of the target at a predefined rate.
This means that the higher the price of the acquiring firm’s stock on the agreement date, the fewer the
t
o
number of shares that must be issued to purchase the target firm. Prior literature shows that such a
negative relationship betwenen bidders’ stock price and shares issued in the transaction leads bidders to
increase their own premerger stock price to lower the target’s acquisition cost. For instance, Erickson
and Wang (1999), Lotuis (2004) and Higgins (2013) find that acquiring firms tend to manipulate
n
total accruals upwards in the periods prior to the merger announcement, while He, Liu, Netter, and Shu
(2020) find thiat bidders significantly manage down analyst earnings forecasts prior to merger
r
announcements. Overall, prior research provides strong evidence that the information environment
p
around stock-for-stock mergers is characterized for being highly uncertain and subject to opportunistic
e
manipulation.

2.1. Stock-for-stock M&As and earnings management
e
The total number of shares issued by the acquiring firm is determined by an exchange ratio, which is the
number of shares of the acquiring firm'sp stock to be issued for each share of the target's stock, as
negotiated by the acquirer and the target. For example, the exchange ratio is four if the acquirer agrees
to exchange four of its stocks for onet target firm share. This ratio is defined such that target shareholders
o
generally obtain a significant premium above the current market price (Erickson and Wang, 1999). In
order to get to the exchangne ratio, the acquiring firm usually first defines the offer to the target firm by

t
firm’s shares exchanged for each of the target’s shares is then calculated based on the acquiring firm’s
n
stock price near the merger agreement date.2 As a result, there exists a negative relationship between the
i
price paid for the target and the bidder’s stock value.

e
Instead of investigating the manipulation of quantitative financial information, we examine whether
w

disclosures and test whether stock-for-stock acquirers, manipulate this content, relative to cash and hybrid
e
acquirors. This focus on corporate narratives over numbers is supported by strong evidence that
i

v

e

r

e

e
value for stakeholders, with its significance increasing with the level of information asymmetry between
p

the tone of earnings press releases around earnings announcements.
