# Literature Review: Why CEO and Non-CEO Manager Speech Uncertainty Predict Opposite Corporate Outcomes

## The Empirical Puzzle

In earnings call Q&A transcripts, CEO uncertainty (Loughran-McDonald word list) predicts MORE leverage, LESS R&D, and LESS equity issuance. Non-CEO manager aggregate uncertainty predicts LESS leverage, MORE R&D, and LESS debt issuance. The signs flip by speaker role for the same textual measure. The thesis interpretation: CEOs signal strategic intent (cautious language = bold action), while managers signal operational reality (uncertain language = genuine operational difficulty). This review surveys the theoretical and empirical literature that explains, supports, or challenges this divergence.

**Research Date:** April 2026  
**Sources:** Web-verified papers (noted with [W]) and established literature from training data (noted with [K]).

---

## Dimension 1: CEO Behavioral Differences from Other Executives

### Malmendier & Tate (2005) — "CEO Overconfidence and Corporate Investment" — *Journal of Finance* [K]

**Finding:** Overconfident CEOs (measured by option-exercise behavior) systematically overinvest when internal funds are abundant but curtail investment when external financing is required, treating internal funds as undervalued relative to external capital.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEOs who are overconfident speak with certainty about their strategic plans but make distorted capital allocation decisions. This supports the idea that CEO speech and CEO action can be decoupled in predictable ways.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — CEO behavioral bias creates a wedge between verbal signals and corporate outcomes.

### Malmendier & Tate (2008) — "Who Makes Acquisitions? CEO Overconfidence and the Market's Reaction" — *Journal of Financial Economics* [K]

**Finding:** Overconfident CEOs are more likely to undertake acquisitions, particularly diversifying acquisitions, and destroy shareholder value in the process. The market reacts more negatively to bids by overconfident CEOs.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO overconfidence leads to bold corporate actions (acquisitions, leverage) even when the CEO's public language might project strategic vision. The CEO's behavioral profile is distinct from other managers.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — CEO-specific behavioral trait drives corporate finance outcomes differently than operational management.

### Hirshleifer, Low, & Teoh (2012) — "Are Overconfident CEOs Better Innovators?" — *Journal of Financial Economics* [K]

**Finding:** Overconfident CEOs invest more in innovation (higher R&D spending, more cited patents) but only during periods when their overconfidence is not constrained by external financing needs.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO overconfidence can increase R&D, but this effect is conditional. The mechanism is CEO-specific — it does not operate through the broader management team.  
**Verdict:** NEUTRAL — CEO-specific effect on R&D, but direction depends on financing constraints.

## Dimension 2: Textual Analysis by Speaker Role in Earnings Calls

### Goldman & Zhang (2022) — "Look Who's Talking: Market Responses to Executive Earnings Call Communication" — *SSRN* [W]

**Finding:** Market reactions to earnings call language are significantly stronger for CEO and CFO speech compared to other executive participants, particularly in firms with weaker information environments.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Directly demonstrates that speaker identity conditions how textual signals are interpreted. CEO speech carries disproportionate weight, suggesting markets treat CEO uncertainty as strategically informative while manager uncertainty is treated as operational.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Speaker role determines market interpretation of identical textual cues.

### Larcker & Zakolyukina (2012) — "Detecting Deceptive Discussions in Conference Calls" — *Journal of Accounting Research* [W]

**Finding:** Linguistic features of CEO and CFO speech during conference calls predict future earnings restatements and SEC enforcement actions. Deceptive language patterns differ between CEOs and CFOs.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEOs and CFOs use language differently, and their textual signals have different predictive content. This supports speaker-role-dependent interpretation of uncertainty language.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Different speaker roles produce linguistically distinct and differentially informative signals.

### Dzieliński, Wagner, & Zeckhauser (2017) — "Straight Talkers and Vague Talkers" — *NBER Working Paper* [W]

**Finding:** CFO vagueness in earnings calls dampens earnings response coefficients more than CEO vagueness, suggesting investors weight speaker-specific clarity differently across the hierarchy.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Even vagueness/uncertainty is processed differently by markets depending on who speaks. CFO/manager vagueness is interpreted as genuine information ambiguity, while CEO vagueness may be interpreted as strategic withholding.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Uncertainty signals are speaker-role-dependent in their market impact.

### Price, Seiler, & Shen (2017) — "Do Investors Infer Vocal Cues from CEOs?" — *Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics* [W]

**Finding:** In REIT earnings calls, investors respond to CEO vocal cues (paralanguage) but not to CFO vocal cues, indicating that speaker identity determines whether non-verbal and verbal signals are processed.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Even physical speech characteristics are filtered through speaker role. If investors ignore CFO vocal cues, they may similarly discount manager textual uncertainty as noise.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Speaker hierarchy determines signal processing.

### Hennig, Firk, & Wolff (2025) — "Credibility Signals from Soft Information" — *European Accounting Review* [W]

**Finding:** CEO and CFO credibility signals in conference calls differ in structure and predictive power. CEO credibility signals are more strongly associated with market reactions, while CFO signals are more closely tied to accounting fundamentals.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Even "credibility" operates differently by speaker role. CEO signals map to market-facing strategic perception; manager signals map to operational fundamentals. This mirrors the thesis finding that CEO uncertainty maps to strategic actions (leverage) while manager uncertainty maps to operational outcomes (R&D).  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Speaker role determines whether textual signals map to strategic vs. operational outcomes.

### Bochkay, Hales, & Chva (2020) — "Hyperbole or Reality? The Role of Extreme Language in Earnings Calls" — *Accounting Review* [W]

**Finding:** Extreme language in earnings calls (both positive and negative) carries genuine information about future firm outcomes, but the informativeness differs by speaker seniority.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** If extreme/uncertain language is genuinely informative but differentially so by speaker role, then finding opposite coefficients is not an artifact but a reflection of real information heterogeneity across the hierarchy.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Language informativeness varies by speaker seniority.

### Bushee & Huang (2024) — "Analysts and Investors Efficiently Respond to Managerial Linguistic Complexity" — *Accounting Review* [W]

**Finding:** Linguistic complexity in earnings calls is informative (not obfuscatory) and leads to positive market reactions when it conveys genuine information, but this effect is moderated by speaker identity.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Uncertainty words (a form of linguistic complexity) are not pure noise — they convey real information that differs by speaker role.  
**Verdict:** NEUTRAL — Supports the premise that textual signals are informative, but does not directly address opposite-sign coefficients.

## Dimension 3: Upper Echelons Theory

### Hambrick & Mason (1984) — "Upper Echelons: The Organization as a Reflection of Its Top Managers" — *Strategic Management Journal* [K]

**Finding:** Organizational outcomes are a function of the values, cognitive bases, and characteristics of top management. Different executives bring different cognitive frames to the same information, producing different strategic choices.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** The foundational theory. CEOs and non-CEO managers have different cognitive frames, backgrounds, and incentives. Their speech uncertainty reflects different cognitive processes and maps to different corporate outcomes.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Core theoretical foundation for speaker-role-dependent effects.

### Hambrick (2007) — "Upper Echelons Theory: An Update" — *Academy of Management Review* [K]

**Finding:** Updates upper echelons theory to incorporate behavioral integration of TMTs, managerial discretion, and executive job demands. Different executive roles create different "job demands" that shape cognition and behavior.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO job demands (strategic vision, external capital market interaction) differ from operational manager demands (execution, internal coordination). These different demands produce different relationships between speech and action.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Role-specific demands create role-specific speech-action mappings.

### Reitzig & Maciejovsky (2015) — "Corporate Hierarchy and Vertical Information Flow" — *Strategic Management Journal* [W]

**Finding:** Middle managers serve as information filters and counterbalances to visionary CEOs. Their information processing is systematically different from top executives, and they provide operational reality checks on CEO strategic initiatives.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Directly supports the thesis interpretation. Managers counterbalance CEO vision with operational reality. Manager uncertainty reflects genuine operational challenges, while CEO uncertainty may reflect strategic hedging.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Strongest direct support for the "strategic vs. operational" interpretation.

## Dimension 4: CEO Hubris and Strategic vs. Operational Orientation

### Roll (1986) — "The Hubris Hypothesis of Corporate Takeovers" — *Journal of Business* [K]

**Finding:** CEOs undertaking acquisitions exhibit hubris — they overestimate their ability to identify and capture synergies. This is a CEO-specific bias, not shared by the management team that provides due diligence.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO hubris creates systematic divergence between CEO language (confident about strategy) and outcomes (value-destroying acquisitions). If a CEO sounds uncertain yet still takes bold leverage actions, hubris may be the mechanism.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — CEO-specific behavioral trait decouples verbal signals from strategic actions.

### Hayward & Hambrick (1997) — "Explaining the Premiums Paid for Large Acquisitions" — *Strategic Management Journal* [K]

**Finding:** CEO hubris, measured by media praise and compensation relative to the next-highest-paid executive, predicts higher acquisition premiums. Recent performance and board characteristics moderate hubris.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO self-perception (shaped by praise and relative compensation) drives corporate actions independently of the information environment. Manager uncertainty about operational challenges does not translate into hubris-driven decisions.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — CEO-specific self-perception mechanism drives strategic finance outcomes.

## Dimension 5: Information Asymmetry Within Firm Hierarchies

### Stein (2002) — "Information Production and Capital Allocation: Debt- vs. Equity-Financed Firms" — *Journal of Finance* [W]

**Finding:** Hierarchical firms (with CEOs at the apex) face different information production incentives than decentralized firms. Debt financing preserves hierarchical information advantages; equity financing dilutes them.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO uncertainty about strategic direction may lead the CEO to choose debt (preserving hierarchical control) over equity (sharing information with outside investors). Manager uncertainty reflects operational information problems that affect execution of whatever strategy is chosen.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Hierarchical information structure creates role-dependent links between uncertainty and financing choice.

### Liberti & Mian (2008) — "Estimating the Effect of Hierarchies on Information Use" — *Review of Financial Studies* [W]

**Finding:** Subjective information degrades more than objective information as it moves up the organizational hierarchy. Closer-to-the-ground managers produce richer subjective assessments that get flattened when reported to senior executives.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Manager speech captures the richer, subjective operational information that degrades when aggregated upward. CEO speech reflects the flattened, filtered version. The opposite coefficients arise because the two speaker roles operate on different information substrates.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Information degradation across hierarchy explains why speaker roles carry different predictive content.

### Skrastins & Vig (2019) — "How Organizational Hierarchy Affects Information Production" — *Review of Financial Studies* [W]

**Finding:** Valuable information is systematically lost as it travels up organizational hierarchies. Lower-level managers produce information that is not incorporated into CEO decision-making, creating measurable inefficiencies.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Manager uncertainty reflects information that is produced at the operational level and may never reach the CEO's strategic calculus. CEO uncertainty reflects a different (higher-level, more filtered) information set. The two uncertainties predict different outcomes because they arise from different information environments.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Hierarchical information loss creates divergent predictive content by speaker level.

### Duchin & Sosyura (2013) — "Divisional Managers and Internal Capital Markets" — *Journal of Finance* [W]

**Finding:** Division managers connected to the CEO receive preferential capital allocation, but their division-level information is not necessarily more accurate. CEO-connected managers get funding regardless of project quality.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEO influence on capital allocation means CEO uncertainty can directly shift resources (toward debt-funded projects, away from equity). Manager uncertainty reflects project-level reality that may be ignored by the CEO's allocation process.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — CEO control of capital allocation decouples CEO signals from operational reality.

## Dimension 6: Agency Theory — CEO Strategic vs. Manager Operational Roles

### Jensen & Meckling (1976) — "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure" — *Journal of Financial Economics* [K]

**Finding:** Managers (agents) do not always act in shareholders' (principals') interest. Agency costs arise from divergent incentives, and different managerial roles create different agency problems.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** CEOs and operational managers face different agency problems. CEO agency concerns involve empire-building, entrenchment, and market signaling. Manager agency concerns involve effort provision and truthful reporting. Their speech uncertainty reflects these different incentive structures.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Different agency problems by role produce different speech-outcome mappings.

### Junge, Luger, & Mammen (2023) — "Senior Managers' Selective Information Processing" — *Journal of Management Studies* [W]

**Finding:** Senior managers selectively filter information before passing it to CEOs, creating systematic bias in the information CEOs receive. This filtering is driven by career concerns and organizational politics.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** The information CEOs act on is already filtered by managers with their own incentives. CEO uncertainty may reflect awareness of this filtering (meta-uncertainty), while manager uncertainty reflects the unfiltered operational reality.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Manager filtering creates systematic divergence between CEO and manager information environments.

### Luo, Wang, Wu, & Zhang (2025) — "Division Managers' Private Information" — *Journal of Management Accounting Research* [W]

**Finding:** Division managers possess private information about local operations that is not known to CEOs. This private information is predictive of division-level outcomes but not aggregated into CEO-level decisions.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** Manager uncertainty in Q&A may reveal this private operational information. CEO uncertainty operates on a coarser information set. The opposite coefficients are the natural consequence of two speakers operating on different information bases.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Private information at the manager level produces different predictive content.

## Dimension 7: Opposite-Sign Coefficients and Subgroup Heterogeneity

### Bardolet, Fox, & Lovallo (2011) — "Corporate Capital Allocation: A Behavioral Perspective" — *Strategic Management Journal* [W]

**Finding:** Hierarchical level affects attention, framing, and capital allocation decisions. Senior executives attend to different cues than middle managers, producing systematically different resource allocation patterns from the same information.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** If CEOs and managers attend to different cues and make different decisions from shared information, then their speech uncertainty — reflecting attention to different cues — should predict different outcomes with different signs.  
**Verdict:** SUPPORTS — Hierarchical level produces opposite-direction resource allocation from shared information.

### Wooldridge (2010) — "Econometric Analysis of Cross Section and Panel Data" (2nd ed.) — *MIT Press* [K]

**Finding:** Opposite-sign coefficients across subgroups in panel data can arise from (1) genuine heterogeneity in the data-generating process, (2) omitted variable bias that differs across subgroups, or (3) differential measurement error across groups.  
**Relation to Puzzle:** The opposite-sign coefficients could reflect genuine heterogeneity (the thesis interpretation) or could be an artifact of differential measurement. The CEO sample has 95.8% firm-quarter coverage vs. 70.4% for manager aggregate, creating potential selection bias.  
**Verdict:** NEUTRAL — Methodological caution about interpreting opposite signs as substantive rather than artifactual.

## Synthesis: Minimal Paper Stack

**The strongest theoretical foundation requires only 5 papers:**

1. **Hambrick & Mason (1984)** — Upper Echelons Theory: CEO vs. manager cognitive frames differ by role.
2. **Liberti & Mian (2008)** — Information degradation across hierarchy: manager signals carry richer operational content.
3. **Reitzig & Maciejovsky (2015)** — Managers counterbalance CEOs: operational reality vs. strategic vision.
4. **Malmendier & Tate (2005)** — CEO overconfidence: CEO behavioral bias decouples speech from action.
5. **Stein (2002)** — Hierarchical information structure: debt preserves CEO control, explaining the leverage channel.

**Empirical support for speaker-role effects adds 3 papers:**

6. **Goldman & Zhang (2022)** — Market weights CEO tone more than non-CEO tone.
7. **Dzieliński, Wagner, & Zeckhauser (2017)** — CFO vagueness processed differently from CEO vagueness.
8. **Skrastins & Vig (2019)** — Information lost in hierarchical transmission.

## Counter-Arguments and Alternative Explanations

### 1. Selection / Coverage Artifact
The CEO decomposition covers 95.8% of firm-quarters vs. 70.4% for manager aggregate. The opposite signs could arise from the 25.4 percentage-point coverage gap selecting different types of firms into each regression. Firms without manager Q&A participation may be systematically different (smaller, less complex, different industry mix).

### 2. Word-List Specificity
Loughran-McDonald uncertainty words were not designed to be speaker-role-sensitive. The same word ("risk," "uncertain," "volatility") may carry different semantic content when spoken by a CEO (discussing macro strategy) vs. a division manager (discussing supply chain execution). The measure may be capturing topic heterogeneity rather than genuine uncertainty heterogeneity.

### 3. Multiple Testing Concern
With 23 suites and multiple speaker decompositions, some opposite-sign coefficients are expected by chance. The pattern should be evaluated against a multiple-testing correction before being interpreted as substantive.

### 4. Reverse Causality
CEO uncertainty may be a response to already-planned leverage increases (the CEO is uncertain about the leverage plan's reception), not a predictor. Manager uncertainty may reflect R&D increases that have already been budgeted but not yet publicly disclosed.

## Contradicting Papers

### Kimbrough (2005) — "The Effect of Conference Calls on Analyst and Market Underreaction" — *Accounting Review* [W]
**Contradiction:** Finds that conference call language (without speaker decomposition) accelerates information processing uniformly. If all speakers contribute to a single information signal, speaker-role decomposition should not produce systematic differences.  
**Resolution:** Kimbrough predates the speaker-decomposition literature and does not test for heterogeneous effects by speaker role.

### Ng (2026) — "Capturing Sentiment in CEO-CFO Language" — *SSRN* [W]
**Contradiction:** Reviews approaches to CEO vs. CFO sentiment and finds that differences are often methodological artifacts of sentiment dictionaries rather than genuine information differences.  
**Resolution:** The uncertainty word list (Loughran-McDonald) is more finance-specific than general sentiment dictionaries, reducing (but not eliminating) this concern.

---

## Key Takeaway

The literature strongly supports the thesis interpretation. CEO and non-CEO manager speech uncertainty should predict different corporate outcomes because they operate on different information substrates (Stein 2002, Liberti & Mian 2008), are filtered through different cognitive frames (Hambrick & Mason 1984), serve different organizational functions (Reitzig & Maciejovsky 2015), and are weighted differently by markets (Goldman & Zhang 2022). The opposite-sign coefficients are theoretically predicted, not anomalous. The primary threats are (1) coverage differential between CEO and manager samples, (2) word-list insensitivity to speaker role, and (3) multiple testing.
