# Speaker notes, REV22 main deck, slides 1 to 13

Full spoken scripts. Written to be spoken and rehearsed until they are yours, not
read off the page. Word counts are measured, not chosen: see
`SPEAKER_NOTES_BUDGET.md`.

Lines beginning with `>` are stage directions. They are not spoken and cost no
words.

## Slide 1

A firm has agreed to buy another company. The deal is not public yet. But the
quarterly earnings call still happens, and the chief executive still takes
questions. This talk is about the words in those answers. Not the prepared
script: the unrehearsed part. What I found is a pattern in that language. It is a
description of a regularity, not a claim about cause.

## Slide 2

Start with the bind the firm is in. The company is sitting on material
information, meaning information important enough to move what investors think.
In the United States there is no rule forcing it to announce merger talks. So
staying quiet is legal.

But the earnings call is scheduled. It happens anyway. And once the chief
executive opens his mouth, a second rule applies: what he says cannot be
misleading.

Look at what that leaves him. He cannot confirm the deal. He cannot deny it
either, because denial would be a lie. He cannot skip the call. The only move
left is to answer around the question.

This is not a theory about honesty. It is a constraint about what is sayable.

That is the prediction. While the deal is still private, the answers should get
more hedged, more qualified, less certain. And that should show up in the words
themselves.

## Slide 3

Two things happen at different times, and that gap is what I use. The
announcement is when the information becomes public. Completion is when the money
actually leaves. Those are separate dates, often months apart.

So the deal runs on two clocks. Information becomes public at announcement. Cash
moves at completion. Neither clock is something I set. Both come from the deal
itself.

Now the second contrast. Cash deals and stock deals sit in the same disclosure
situation. Both are private until announced. But a stock deal adds something. The
buyer is paying with its own shares, so it has a reason to care about its share
price in the run-up.

That makes cash the relatively cleaner setting to look at. I want to be careful
here. Cleaner is not clean. Firms choose how to pay, and that choice is not
random. So the comparison motivates the question. It does not settle it.

## Slide 4

Three questions, and the rest of the talk answers them in order.

First: does the language change before a cash acquisition is announced? That is
the run-up question.

Second: if it changes, when does it stop? Does the language settle at
announcement while the cash moves later, at completion?

Third: is this stronger for cash than for stock? And here the test has to be a
direct comparison of the two, not two separate results looked at side by side.

One thing about all three. Every test is a within-firm comparison, which is to
say each firm is compared with itself in its own other quarters. That is what
fixed effects do: they strip out anything about a firm that does not change. And
every answer is a descriptive result: a measured pattern rather than a cause.
Nothing here identifies a mechanism. I will say that again at the end.

## Slide 5

Where this sits. There is good work reading the language of earnings calls. Some
of it reads managed tone. Some reads the vocabulary of strategy. Some reads what
prices do around the call.

One distinction runs through all of it. The prepared remarks are written in
advance. The answers are not, and the answers are where I look.

What I did not find was work reading the uncertainty in a chief executive's
unscripted answers, in the specific window when an acquisition is agreed but not
yet announced.

That is the cell this thesis occupies. I say to our knowledge, and I mean it as
written. It is a claim about what I searched, not a claim that nothing exists.

The contribution is narrow on purpose. One measure, one window, one comparison.

## Slide 6

Where the evidence comes from.

The starting panel is 88,205 earnings calls from 1,884 United States public
firms, running from 2002 to 2018. Financial firms and utilities are excluded.

Then it narrows, and the reason matters. The outcome I need is not something the
transcript records directly. I have to build it, and building it takes repeated
calls from the same chief executive. So I need to know who is speaking. I also
need enough calls from that person to model how they normally speak. Anyone with
fewer than five calls drops out. Executive compensation coverage and the inputs
the measure needs narrow it further.

The focal test runs on 27,622 firm-quarters, which is one firm observed in one
quarter, across 1,248 firms.

Be straight about what that selection does. What survives tilts toward larger,
better-covered firms with stable leadership. So when I say this describes United
States public firms, I mean a particular slice of them. The smallest firms are
not in here at all. That is a limit on who these results describe, and I would
rather state it now than defend it later.

## Slide 7

The measure, because everything downstream depends on it.

Start with the raw version. Take the chief executive's answers in the
question-and-answer section. Count the share of words drawn from a fixed list of
uncertainty terms. That is a blunt count.

The problem is that people differ. Some executives always hedge. That is
personality, not information. If I compare across people, I am mostly measuring
personality.

So I remove the predictable part. Residualization is removing what a model can
already explain. What remains is the residual: the part of this call's
uncertainty that the speaker's usual style does not account for.

Three things this is not. It is not raw uncertainty. It is not a window into what
the chief executive privately knows. And it is a built outcome rather than an
observed one. That two-step construction leaves extra uncertainty in the
statistics, and I carry that as a limitation.

What it is: a proxy for how uncertain this particular call sounded, once the
speaker's own baseline is taken out.

## Slide 8

The first result.

In the quarter before a cash acquisition is announced, residual uncertainty in
the chief executive's answers goes up. The coefficient, which is the estimated
size of that shift, is plus 0.0461. Its standard error is 0.0172, and the
standard error is how uncertain the estimate itself is. The two-tailed p-value is
0.0074, which tests departures in either direction.

In plain terms, if there were no shift at all, a result this size or larger would
turn up in well under one percent of samples.

Now the part that actually matters, which is how big this is.

The usual spread of this measure across calls is 0.301. Against that, the shift
is about 15.3 percent of one standard deviation. So it is not a dramatic effect.
It is a modest, statistically clear shift.

One caution on the bar you can see on the slide. That is an approximate 95
percent range, and a confidence interval is a plausible range around the
estimate. The slide says derived, and it means it. I computed it from the
estimate and its standard error. The thesis prints the estimate and the standard
error, not the range.

The sample is 27,622 firm-quarters from 1,248 firms. Standard errors are grouped
by firm, because one firm's calls are not independent of each other.

And note what the comparison is. It is the same firm in its own other quarters,
not this firm against other firms. That matters, because executives differ
enormously in how they speak.

The framing stays descriptive. This is a within-firm pattern in language before
an announcement. It is not evidence that the deal made the chief executive talk
that way.

## Slide 9

The second result, and this is the one I would push on if I were you.

The design is an event study, which compares outcomes across stages around an
event. Four stages: two quarters before announcement, the quarter immediately
before, the window after announcement but before closing, and after closing.

Take the language first. Two quarters out, the estimate is 0.0068 with a standard
error of 0.0178. Nothing detected. One quarter out, it is 0.0473.

Say that carefully. It is the same rise as the previous slide, but it is not the
same number. This is a second estimate, on a matched sample of 28,102
firm-quarters, so it lands close to the first without being identical.

Then in the announced-but-not-closed window it falls to 0.0018.

Be careful how I say what happens next, because there are two different claims
here and only one of them is supported.

What is supported is a decline. The drop from the quarter before to the announced
window is 0.0455, and that difference is significant.

What is not supported is equivalence. A small estimate with a wide error bar is a
failure to detect. It does not establish that the effect has gone away.

The same discipline applies to the flat quarter two out. That is no detected
elevation. It does not establish that the earlier quarters are clean.

Now cash, on the lower panel. Cash does not fall at announcement. It falls after
completion, and that difference is 0.0210, which is significant.

One honest caution there. Part of that drop is mechanical. Paying for a company
with cash reduces the cash you hold.

So the two run on different clocks. Information settles when it becomes public.
Money moves when it is paid.

## Slide 10

The third result, and the one with the sharpest trap in it.

On the left you see two panels side by side. Cash acquirers show the run-up.
Stock acquirers show an estimate that is negative and imprecise.

Here is the trap. It is tempting to say cash is significant, stock is not,
therefore they differ. That is not a test. Two separate results looked at
separately tell you nothing about whether they differ from each other.

So I ran the test directly. The Wald test is a direct test of whether two
estimates differ. Put both in one regression and ask for the difference. It is
plus 0.0983, with a standard error of 0.0476 and a p-value of 0.039.

That is the result. The difference itself is significant, not just one side of
it.

What it supports is concentration in cash. What it does not support is a cash-
only effect, and I will not claim one. It also does not tell you why.

And I would call this the most fragile of the three findings. The stock estimate
is imprecise, so what is established is that the two differ, not that stock is
flat.

## Slide 11

Three contributions, stated as what they add rather than as a second run at the
results.

The first is where I look. Prior work reads the language of calls in many ways.
This puts a residual uncertainty measure inside the specific window where a deal
is agreed and not yet public.

The second is how I test payment method. The cash and stock comparison is done as
one direct difference, not as two separate results held up next to each other.

The third is what it connects. Work on withholding private information and work
on what happens around transactions mostly sit apart. The private-to-public
moment is a place they meet.

All three describe a regularity. None of them identifies a channel, and I am not
claiming one.

## Slide 12

Four limits, and I would rather state them than be walked into them.

The first is what kind of claim this is. It is a within-firm pattern, not cause
and effect.

The second is who it describes. Larger, better-covered firms with stable
leadership, for one country and one period.

The third is timing. I know when a deal was announced. I do not know when the
talks started. So the quarter I flag is a proxy for a window, not confirmed
exposure to a negotiation.

The fourth is measurement. A fixed word list is a crude way to read uncertainty,
and the stock comparison group is imperfect for the reasons on slide 3.

## Slide 13

One sentence to take away.

When an acquisition moves from private to public, the unscripted part of the
earnings call carries a trace of it, and that trace is readable.

It appears in the quarter before the announcement. It is no longer detectable
once the deal is public. And it is stronger for cash deals than for stock deals.

It is a pattern in language. It is not a mechanism, and it is not something
anyone could trade on. That is the whole claim, and I have tried to keep it that
size.

Thank you. I am happy to take questions.
